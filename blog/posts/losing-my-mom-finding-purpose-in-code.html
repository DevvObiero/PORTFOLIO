From Losing My Mom to Finding Purpose in Code: My Journey as a Self-Taught Developer

This one is more personal than what I usually write here. It's the story behind why I code at all.

23 October 2026 · 5 min read

Most of what I write here is about code: automations, landing pages, how this blog works. This one is about why I code at all. I've started it a few times and deleted it, because the honest answer runs through the hardest part of my life. But it's also the most important thing I could tell you about how I got here, so I'm going to try.

Learning alone

I taught myself to code. [WHEN AND HOW YOU STARTED: THE YEAR, WHAT YOU LEARNED ON, WHAT YOUR DAYS LOOKED LIKE] There was no bootcamp, no mentor, and nobody in the room to ask when I got stuck. It was me, a laptop and whatever free tutorials I could find.

The tutorials were confusing in a very particular way. Each one made sense while I was watching it, and stopped making sense the moment I closed the video and opened an empty file. I'd follow along building a to-do app, feel great about it, then try to build something of my own and have no idea where to start.

Progress was slow. Things that look simple to me now took weeks to understand. [SOMETHING THAT TOOK YOU A LONG TIME TO "GET", E.G. A CONCEPT OR A BUG YOU STILL REMEMBER] And underneath all of it was imposter syndrome, constantly. It felt like everyone else online was further ahead, faster, more naturally gifted. I'd compare my early attempts with other people's best work and feel like a fraud for even trying. I still feel that sometimes. I've just learned it isn't a reliable narrator.

The main reason I started was my mom.

Then I lost her. [OPTIONAL: WHEN, OR ANYTHING ELSE ABOUT THIS YOU WANT TO SAY. IT'S COMPLETELY FINE TO LEAVE THIS OUT.]

I don't want to dress this part up. For a while, it felt like I hadn't only lost her; I'd lost my purpose too. So much of what I was working towards had been for her. Without her, the goal didn't make sense any more, and neither did the work.

Finding purpose in code again

What I can say is that, slowly, coding became the thing that gave me purpose again. It didn't happen overnight, and I didn't plan it. [WHAT BROUGHT YOU BACK TO IT: A PROJECT, A PERSON, A MOMENT]

Somewhere along the way, I fell in love with frontend development. I love that you can see what you're building. You change a line of CSS, refresh, and the page is different. I love making something feel smooth on a phone, and the small details nobody notices unless they're wrong. It's problem-solving that also cares about how things feel to the person using them.

The work also gave my days a shape when I needed one. A bug is a problem that can actually be solved, and at a time when not much made sense, that mattered more than I can easily explain.

The door that wouldn't open

Then came the job search, which was hard in a completely different way.

Interviews were rare. I'm in Kenya, and so many of the roles I applied for, including remote ones, came with the same line: candidates must already have the right to work in a particular country, and the company won't sponsor a visa. "Remote" often turned out to mean "remote, as long as you live here". I'd find a role I was a good fit for, read to the bottom of the listing, and close the tab. Then GoHighLevel

Then I landed a GoHighLevel role. [HOW YOU FOUND IT, AND WHAT THE WORK WAS] Then another one. Then a third.

It wasn't the path I'd pictured. I'd imagined myself somewhere writing React all day. But the work used everything I'd been teaching myself: building funnel pages that load fast on phones, writing custom code and webhooks, and thinking through automations the way I think through the state of a user interface. I wrote more about that side of it in What I've Learned Building on GoHighLevel.

If you're in Kenya and learning to code right now

If any part of this sounds like where you are, here's what I'd want you to hear:

  • It's a long road. Longer than the videos and the success stories make it look. That's normal. It isn't a sign you're not cut out for this.
  • Keep building. Real things for real people (a relative's business, your church, a friend's side hustle) will teach you more than one more tutorial, and they give you something to show.
  • Read the location line, but don't let it stop you. Some doors are closed because of where you live. Plenty aren't. Look for companies and clients who hire globally.
  • Specialised tools are doors too. Platforms like GoHighLevel have businesses that need people who can build on them, and many of those businesses care about whether you can do the work, not where you live.
  • Find people. Doing it completely alone was the hardest part for me. [COMMUNITIES, PEOPLE OR RESOURCES IN KENYA YOU'D RECOMMEND]
  • Your reason is allowed to change. Mine had to. Finding a new one doesn't mean the old one stopped mattering.

It's a long road. Keep building. The right door opens: maybe not the one you were pushing on, and probably not when you expected. But it opens.
